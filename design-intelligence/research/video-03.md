## Teaching format

A before/after rebuild narrated through named exemplar sites first ("let's learn from X"),
then applied to his own design segment by segment. Each of the 4 hack chapters ends with the
same artifact evolving on screen; fast cuts, motion shown in short before/after bursts.
Alchemy of the video: the hero grows from a gray stock-image layout → centered text with
doodle illustrations → animated doodles → headline text-animation loop → friendly copy →
fully thematized site with green palette and a themed 404.

## Lesson log

### L03-1 Countering "corporate AI mush": fun and curiosity are conversion-relevant
- Problem [transcript 0:01–0:16]: "Playful websites are slowly being replaced with corporate
  AI mush... Somewhere along the way modern became a synonym for sterile. Like Mailchimp
  pre-2020 and Mailchimp now. Nothing is wrong with it. It just doesn't have the fun, the
  curiosity that well-designed websites do."
- Decision: rebuild the sterile page ("Turn it into something delightful for the user",
  0:19–0:21) by importing the qualities the exemplars have — context-rich decor, playful
  illustration, motion, personality.
- Evidence: frame `t00m00m07` (the "mush" reference: Dropbox — plain white login card and a
  standard homepage with small centered copy + generic screenshot) vs later frames of the
  same-category revamp (`t00m06m27` final: themed green Breakout hero).
- Effect: sets the video's bar — "a playful, bespoke masterpiece" (6:26–6:29).
- Fails: enterprise/compliance contexts where playful signals unprofessionalism (implied;
  his own invoicing exemplar is the "professional" end of the dial, L03-5).
- Transfer: audit a landing page as if Mailchimp's 2020→now comparison had happened to it;
  reinstate one layer of personality per pass (illustration, motion, copy, error pages).

### L03-2 More is more: elements are context, not clutter — don't strip the busy hero
- Problem [transcript 0:24–0:34]: busy-looking heroes tempt deletion. "There are a lot of
  things on the screen. However, if we remove them, it doesn't make it clearer. It just
  makes it feel empty."
- Decision: keep every element that explains the product. On the Actual invoicing hero, the
  scattered items "give context" — "the money, the invoice, and the crypto. Without even
  reading anything, we start to get a pretty good idea of what the product is for" and on
  Family's crypto wallet "everything else, like the coins, Ethereum, and fun character are
  there to give context to what the landing page is about."
- Evidence: `t00m00m26` (Actual: centered "Get paid same day / …flexible invoice on the
  planet / Create invoice in seconds" surrounded by chat bubbles, invoices, oversized
  dollar bill, coins, paperclip, binder clip, logo strip), `t00m00m45` + `t00m01m45`
  (Family: "Your favorite crypto wallet." centered, flanked by cheerful character blobs,
  coins, stars, gems).
- Effect: comprehension before reading (explicit 0:39–0:42).
- Fails: decorative shapes with no semantic tie to the product — the whole lesson depends on
  each element naming something real.
- Transfer: for a hero, list the product's nouns (money, invoices, calendar, links…) and
  scatter them as texture around ONE centered message.

### L03-3 Radial hierarchy for broken-grid layouts: big near the center, sparse at the edges
- Problem [transcript 1:31–1:39]: "Since we're breaking the box and not arranging elements
  in line with the grid, we need to be very selective in how we arrange them. There's a lot
  of eyeballing it."
- Decision: "generally, you want the elements to draw the user's attention to the center.
  That's why in this crypto site, you'll notice that the elements trail off the further you
  get from the center." Also keep "good spacing around the text so that the elements are
  context, not clutter" (1:23–1:30).
- Evidence: `t00m01m45`/`t00m00m45` (Family page: largest characters hugging the headline,
  tiny stars/coins far out), and his own pass `t00m01m08` (Breakout: centered copy block;
  balloon bottom-left, calendar mid-right; the two biggest props at equal distance; tiny
  sparkles at the periphery).
- Effect: a broken grid that still has one gravity point; the eye lands on the offer.
- Transfer: when abandoning the column grid, substitute a radial rule — element size and
  density decay with distance from the message.

### L03-4 One-look test for crossing the clutter line
- Problem [transcript 1:14–1:23]: he literally demonstrates over-filling: "we do have some
  empty space up here. So, if I was to grab another illustration, we could pop it in here,
  but that pushes our design over the line to cluttered."
- Decision: reject the "helpful" addition even when an obvious slot exists; stop at the last
  element that keeps breathing room (pulling back to the assembled state).
- Evidence: `t00m01m08` (without the extra envelope) → `t00m01m20` (with a green envelope
  and letter doodle squeezed beside the headline, buttons double-exposed in the zoom =
  the state he calls cluttered) — then later frames return to the un-cluttered set.
- Effect: whitespace survives the decoration pass.
- Bench rule (explicit 1:23–1:30): "they maintain good spacing around the text so that the
  elements are context, not clutter."
- Transfer: any time a decor idea has an obvious slot, add it, look once, subtract if
  density reads; the judgement artefact is "one look too busy" not the element count.

### L03-5 Calibrate playfulness to the brand — with named anchors on a spectrum
- Problem [transcript 1:55–2:16]: copying the wrong exemplar's vibe.
- Decision: place yourself on a playfulness scale by real sites — "The crypto website is by
  far the most playful with blobs and fun colors, whereas this invoicing website is more on
  the side of professional with more realistic imagery. We probably land somewhere in the
  middle with our doodles." Gumroad's neo-brutalism "probably falls pretty close to our own
  design for playfulness."
- Evidence: `t00m02m13` — his "Playfulness-o-meter" slide: Gumroad shown center, with a
  slider below mapping thumbnails: Family's crypto page on the "very" end, his Breakout page
  mid-late, Actual toward "not very".
- Effect: decor, motion and copy intensity then all get scaled by the same one dial.
- Fails: mixing ends of the dial on one page (blobs + bank-serif realism) — he sorts his own
  references INTO one zone rather than alternating.
- Transfer: before restyling, fix the dial (very playful ↔ corporate) from the product's
  actual emotional stakes, then source references from that zone only.

### L03-6 Motion for delight, not utility — the "should work statically" test
- Problem [transcript 2:50–3:16]: how much motion is too much?
- Decision: none of his examples "need to" animate — "just because things don't have to
  move, doesn't mean they shouldn't. For example, Paste app doesn't need to have all these
  scroll animations. This could be presented statically and get the point across." Daniel's
  portfolio likewise: "It's pretty subtle, but right at the bottom, you can see these images
  rotating in."
- Evidence: `t00m02m56` (Paste: "Limitless clipboard" hero with device mockup + gradient
  headline — content legible in the still frame), `t00m03m05` (Daniel's portfolio: pill nav
  "Work/Story/Process/Connect", illustration card, project card "Ruby → Branding, Web
  design, Development" — the rotating image strip site).
- Effect: motion adds delight only after information integrity exists as a still frame.
- Fails: motion carrying essential information (progressive disclosure with no scroll-
  independent fallback).
- Transfer: screenshot-check every animated section: if the still is incomplete, redesign
  before animating.

### L03-7 Animate illustrations in character with what they depict
- Problem [transcript 3:16–3:21]: standard entrances are boring — "could have no entrance or
  a really robotic fade in. But that's no fun."
- Decision: subject-true motion — star: "rotate and pop into view" (spins in with overshoot);
  hot air balloon: "literally fly in from off the screen and very slowly bob up and down
  once it's in place"; calendar: "move and rotate in".
- Evidence: `t00m03m24` (hero zoomed: star above "Track deadlines." mid-entrance), `t00m03m31`
  (balloon parked left of headline — in-place bobbing state), `t00m03m43` (assembled: balloon,
  star, calendar all settled — the "after" he shows).
- Reasoning: element = object in the world → objects have physical entrances; UI fades are
  for interface, not for diegetic props.
- Effect: the page reads as a scene rather than a layout; entrance timing itself signals
  "(this is a balloon)".
- Fails: overacting the physics on dense/dashboards UI; entrance loops that retrigger on
  every breakpoint change.
- Transfer: inventory the illustrative props and write one physical-style entrance per prop
  (fly/fall/spin/draw-in), then park them into idle micro-motion (bob) — an ideal split
  between entrance and ambiance.

### L03-8 Easing + parallax in the generous margins = perceived 3D without 3D assets
- Problem [transcript 3:53–4:07]: flat scroll on decorated pages; his earlier heavy exemplar
  "crazy backgrounds like this".
- Decision: apply easing from his previous video on swipe effects "to create even more
  natural motion"; and since "we have these elements in our large and fairly spacious
  margins, we can leverage a bit of parallax on scroll to get a much more lifelike feeling
  website without needing to layer in crazy backgrounds."
- Evidence: `t00m03m54` (easing comparison slide: six dashed tracks labeled LINEAR /
  EASE-IN / EASE-OUT / EASE-IN-OUT / GENTLE / QUICK, each ball at a different completion
  point over "4000 ms"), `t00m04m02` (Delassus Group: "Snacking tomatoes" over saturated red
  background with 3D low-poly fruits and a category tab row — the site he credits as feeling
  3D via this method "[plus] some fantastic mouse interactions", 4:00–4:07).
- Effect: same asset count, more depth; motion budget spent on feel not on spectacle.
- Transfer: for wide-margin marketing pages: parallax speed caps of the balloon-scale props,
  plus mouse-followers; produce the 3D feel from layer velocity, not from WebGL scenes.
- [interp] The blurred "crazy backgrounds" clip (3:58–4:00) was not isolated in a frame; the
  claim that Delassus uses parallax+mouse is the creator's reading of the site, not a shown
  mechanism.

### L03-9 Text is ~80% of a website — animate the words' meaning, not the blocks
- Problem [transcript 4:08–4:13]: "because text is like 80% of most websites, it's neither
  fun or interesting" by default.
- Decision: turn key words of the headline into self-explaining micro-widgets, sequenced as
  a narrative on the lines: line one — "start with a simple fade in slide down for track,
  but we'll make deadlines into an actual progress bar that fills up, then rises up and out
  with the word deadline replacing it"; line two — "paid will be replaced with dollar signs
  that bounce and then slide down, and paid will actually drop down"; plus "a little
  checkbox out at the end".
- Evidence: `t00m04m33` (Reboot reference: "Building great sites involves many disciplines.
  Design. Engineering. Branding. Video. 3D. Interaction." where each word has its own
  visual/animation treatment), `t00m04m44` (line 1 mid-cycle: "Track" + a green segmented
  progress bar where "deadlines" was), `t00m04m52` (line 2 mid-cycle: "Get $$$" with green
  dollar signs), `t00m05m00` (line 2 slot empty — post-drop state of the cycle),
  `t00m06m27` (final loop assembled: "Track deadlines. Get paid. ✔").
- Reasoning (explicit 5:00–5:07): "while these effects were pretty simple, combined with the
  motion we set up above, the website really starts to come to life."
- Effect: the value proposition literally performs itself; repeat views keep discovering
  details.
- Fails: animating whole paragraphs; replacing words users need to read on first load (his
  loop settles back to the readable canonical headline).
- Transfer: pick 1–3 load-bearing words in a headline and design a word-swap widget for
  each (progress bar, coin/cash glyph, check, animation) — the loop must land back on plain
  readable text.

### L03-10 One idea per surface: a single effect can carry an all-text page
- Problem [transcript 5:10–5:30]: overstimulated pages.
- Decision: "The goal isn't to be as complex as possible, but to draw attention to the text
  because it's moving." Logan's portfolio: "entirely text except for this one blur effect,
  which reveals some fun message bubbles." Chester's site: "refreshingly simple, but with a
  variety of small hover interactions of pretty much everything means that simplicity isn't
  bland."
- Evidence: `t00m05m17` (Logan Liffick: black page, plain text bio, one blurred project-name
  span + "…" bubble in Latest), `t00m05m26` (Chester: serif copy with a handful of
  black-emphasis words, project cards, cat/climbing photos — one hover interaction "pretty
  much everywhere" per his narration).
- Effect: memorable without noise; each surface earns its one effect.
- Transfer: per page choose a single primary effect (loop, blur-reveal, hover chorus) and
  zero others; resist stacking L03-9 with L03-8 on the same canvas.

### L03-11 Friendly copy is a design decision, not copywriting decoration
- Problem [transcript 5:31–5:42]: stiff corporate phrasing kills the playful frame set by
  visuals.
- Decision: prefer how a friend would say it — "here, how they say they sweat the details
  instead of something like we take pride in our attention to detail. Or look at Basecamp's
  entire website. It's built around natural, easy-to-understand language instead of
  corporate jargon."
- Evidence: `t00m05m38` ("Details that matter. / We sweat the details, no matter how small."
  beside a Family crypto transfer widget "Sending to Benji Taylor / Ethereum"), and his own
  copy pass — in final frames the subtext and 404 line (`t00m06m18`: "Looks like a deadline
  got lost in the wind") adopt first-person, concrete, lightly hedged phrasing.
- Effect: the personality layer survives into the words; brand voice is consistent.
- Transfer: rewrite H2s and helper text as spoken lines (contractions, concrete nouns,
  "we/you"); test by reading aloud against a corporate alternative.

### L03-12 Give every dead-end a themed moment — 404 pages as sanctioned play
- Problem [transcript 5:55–6:03]: error pages are the one page nobody designs.
- Decision: "These are the ultimate time to be quirky and fun because the user literally
  doesn't belong there." Exemplars: Mobbin's 404 "is an interface guessing quiz", Pixar's
  "is a character from one of their movies" (transcript-only; see Unverified), a pizza
  website "automatically redirects you once the snake eats all the pizza from the tree."
  His own 404 ties the theme and keeps a single action.
- Evidence: `t00m06m07` (Mobbin: "Page not found / …Head back to the discover page…"
  with a working quiz card: phone screenshot, "Oh you're a designer? Which app is this
  screen from?" — 1Password/Saturn Calendar/Bears Gratitude/Speechify options, Next, Streak
  1/Best 1), `t00m06m13` (pizza 404: cream background, engraved olive-tree with pizza
  slices, giant "404" numerals), `t00m06m18` (Breakout 404: green field, three balloons,
  "Looks like a deadline got lost in the wind ⏎ / Let's head back to base" pill).
- Effect: an error stop becomes a brand touchpoint; one clear recovery action.
- Transfer: template theme-consistent 404s (image + one joke + one CTA); optionally
  gamify like Mobbin (quiz, streak) if the audience is design-native.
