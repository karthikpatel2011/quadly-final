## Lesson log

### L16-1 Meta: there are only six styles — learnable patterns beat overwhelmed imitation
- Problem [transcript 0:10–0:21]: "you've looked at some really great websites before and
  thought, 'Man, that is so good. I could never build something like that. In fact, I think
  I might actually suck.'"
- Decision: "when you start seeing the patterns of these six styles, it starts to become a
  lot easier to recreate them yourself" (0:22–0:29). Each style is anchored to a famous
  person "to make them easier to remember" (0:36–0:43).
- Evidence: `t00m00m15` — intro skit frame (creator at laptop, yellow caption "THAT IS SO"
  mid-animation over the "I could never" monologue); the five on-screen style cards listed
  in Teaching format.
- Effect: turns admiration into a decoding exercise.
- Transfer: when reverse-engineering any reference site, first bucket it into an archetype,
  then copy its cues.

### L16-2 Style 1 — "The Steve Jobs": the clean-default style (skinny sans, obvious system, white space)
- Problem (clients): "I just want something clean" — the phrase "is exactly what they mean"
  (1:13–1:21).
- Decision [transcript 0:47–1:21]: skinny sans serifs; "spacing and type that follows an
  obvious system. So everything on the page feels organized without feeling too stiff."
- Reasoning (explicit 1:30–1:44): it feels cutting-edge "because of the same reason that
  those spaceship doors in movies feel… really advanced… Removing stuff is what makes it
  feel like the future."
- How-to (1:45–1:56): "you can't be afraid of white space. Just because a section doesn't
  have a lot going on doesn't mean it's not well designed." Closes with the Antoine de
  Saint-Exupéry quote: "Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but
  when there is nothing left to take away" (1:56–2:11).
- Evidence: `t00m00m47` (title card: yellow "THE STEVE JOBS" on black band + haloed Jobs
  holding an iPhone-shaped card); `t00m01m00` — example site (Airform): near-empty white
  viewport, tiny nav (Residential / Multifamily), single "Configure" pill, one short serif-
  free paragraph — extreme restraint; `t00m01m55` — his own minimal portfolio build ("LATEST
  WORK", sparse Milk/Jacket Company rows) showing "don't fear white space" applied;
  `t00m02m03` — Saint-Exupéry quote card.
- Effect: premium calm; matches the "clean" ask precisely.
- Fails: clients who actually need information density (his later styles exist for that).
- Transfer: default heuristic for B2B/professional-services clients.

### L16-3 Style 2 — "The Jensen Huang": buttoned-up SaaS precision (grid, no full-bleed, function over form)
- Problem (clients): SaaS/AI companies courting investors; "they wanna communicate a
  no-fuss stability, who mean business" (3:15–3:20). Persona logic: Jensen's fixed uniform
  = "he doesn't have time to think about outfit variations" (2:52–2:57) — same message:
  building the future > being interesting.
- Decision: "taking the Steve Jobs style and making it even more precise" (3:32) — e.g.
  Melrose AI: "there's no section that goes full width across the screen from all the way
  left to all the way right. Everything is buttoned up" (3:23–3:31). But "that doesn't mean
  there's no style… Even Jensen himself wears a leather jacket" (3:37–3:42): keep one
  deliberate accent of coolness.
- How-to (4:05–4:24): "prioritize function over form. Keep things in a really clear grid.
  Be careful about any loud or distracting design choices. Everything should pull attention
  back to the product… You're not trying to be cool. You're trying to be the next big tech
  breakthrough."
- Evidence: `t00m02m22` (title card); `t00m02m26` (Jensen + NVIDIA logo card); `t00m03m25`
  — Melrose AI site: boxed content column inside a black frame, clear section grid ("Too
  many tools, too much manual work."), partner logo row, orange hiring banner — no full-bleed
  anywhere; `t00m03m40` — Jensen leather-jacket photo collage (the "consistent uniform"
  metaphor).
- Effect: instant credibility with technical/investor audiences.
- Fails: consumer/emotional brands (see Styles 3–5).
- Transfer: for product-led clients, box + grid + one accent; restraint reads as stability.

### L16-4 Style 3 — "The Drew Barrymore": warm, candid, approachable
- Problem (clients): brands that "wanna communicate that they wanna feel approachable, like
  they're down to earth" (6:20–6:26).
- Decision: "sites with this style have almost a… candid feel… like you walked in on a
  private moment… The design doesn't take itself too seriously. There's nothing super bold,
  and there's even a noticeable bounce to things with the animations" (5:39–5:59).
- Example (6:00–6:19): **Fruitful** — "the person in the explainer video is filming it from
  her apartment… and this is for an automated system for managing your money"; "even when I
  click anywhere on the site, these little leaves animate out of it."
- How-to (6:29–6:55): "the main thing that you need to pull this off is good photography,
  the kind that splits the difference between highly produced and highly authentic"; when
  clients bring weak imagery "you can subsidize that by… rounding out the corners of your
  cards or your images or even going with a color palette that has warm tones and a real
  natural feel."
- Evidence: `t00m05m30` (title card); `t00m05m38` — Drew halftone portrait labeled "Girl
  Next Door" / "Non-Judgy Friend"; `t00m06m03` — Fruitful: couch-level webcam-style explainer
  still inside the site ("You need a system" subtitle), laurel badges (Best Planning
  Platform / Excellent 4.8/5); `t00m06m48` — his own Showit build "Gatherwell": cream
  background, rounded photo grid of people at home, "APPLY TO JOIN" dark-green button — the
  rounded+warm subsidization recipe live; `t00m05m41` — warm-beige packaging site (Yucca
  Packaging) shown as B-roll moments after the Drew card.
- Effect: trust through informality; motion personality (leaf bursts) without loudness.
- Fails: luxury/tech-clientele audiences that read warmth as unserious.
- Transfer: rounded corners + warm neutrals = cheapest substitute for editorial-grade
  photography.

### L16-5 Style 4 — "The Zendaya": high-fashion editorial cool (minimal, unconventional, taste-forward)
- Problem (clients): those who "communicate their value by showing people their taste, not
  necessarily by saying 'If you hire us, you'll get XYZ in return.' What you get is
  understood innately from who they are" (8:06–8:20).
- Decision: "high fashion editorial type feel. The imagery, if there is any, has a cinematic
  cologne commercial kind of feel… the design is typically ultra minimal with layouts that
  don't necessarily follow common principles… we know you gotta bounce your eyes to read
  different paragraphs, but hey, we're too busy being cool to worry about it" (7:22–7:45).
- Example (7:52–8:01): **Still Agency** — "the entire homepage is this video that looks like
  an ad for Paris Fashion Week."
- How-to (8:21–8:41): "get really familiar with Swiss design and editorial design. So
  modular layouts are your go-to move, and some of the normal design principles kind of have
  to take a back seat… don't be afraid to scale back the font size or try a layout that's a
  bit unconventional."
- Evidence: `t00m07m07` (title card "THE ZENDAYA"); `t00m07m09`/`t00m07m12` — Zendaya photo
  collage (Bero-brand appearances); `t00m07m55` — Still Agency: full-viewport dark video
  hero, giant "SSTIL" logotype, hairline bracket nav ([ SERVICES ], [ CONTACT ]) —
  cinematic, near-empty; `t00m08m33` — his own editorial build: serif display ("wardrobes
  they actually love.", "My story starts in a small town thrift store…"), offset image
  blocks, tiny red chip CTAs — modular editorial layout in practice.
- Effect: perceived taste/stature; value implied rather than argued.
- Fails: conversion-critical pages (sacrifices some readability by design).
- Transfer: when the client is the product, photography + type carry the pitch; copy yields.

### L16-6 Style 5 — "The Virgil Abloh": the one element you can't ignore (contrast, not bigness)
- Problem (clients): wanting a signature moment — "They want you to see an element and say,
  'That's sick'" (9:20–9:25).
- Decision: "His style isn't about minimalism or maximalism… It's about being something that
  you can't ignore one way or another" (9:05–9:17). The element can be "a font that really
  stands out or a color palette with really high contrast or… some kind of imagery…
  you know it took skill and talent to create" (9:27–9:38).
- Example (9:41–9:55): **Obsidian Assembly** — "the moment that it loads, your eyes are
  instantly pulled to this unique font that's a bit bigger, and this 3D-looking shiny rock."
- How-to (9:59–10:39): don't feel you need stunning visuals in every section/page; "picking
  one cool visual element and just kinda repeating it throughout the site tastefully…
  maybe it's a super big unique font that works more like a design element than it does text
  that you gotta read, or maybe it's a small 3D object that stands out. The point is
  contrast, not bigness. You want people's attention to be drawn to it because it stands
  out, not because it's screaming at you."
- Evidence: `t00m08m50` (title card); `t00m09m00` — Virgil's actual work montage: Murakami
  flower in the Off-White arrows, Off-White "AIR" Jordan 1 with zip-tie, album-cover CD;
  `t00m09m45` — Obsidian Assembly hero: giant elegant serif "Nothing Shown First" layered
  over a glossy 3D obsidian rock on a champagne gradient; `t00m10m25` — his own build
  "Relentless": viewport-filling Anton-type "Determined Focused Relentless" (text-as-design-
  element; right panel visibly set to Font: Anton Normal, Size 300), old logo struck through.
- Effect: one memorable asset carries a whole brand; repetition builds recognition.
- Fails: content-heavy utility pages; more than one hero element (dilutes the effect).
- Transfer: choose a single repeatable signature (type, 3D object, or palette snap) and
  resist adding a second.

### L16-7 Style 6 — "The Christopher Nolan": engineered wow-factor interaction
- Problem (clients): "the clients who say, 'I want a wow factor'" (11:44–11:47) — he notes
  an actual prospect email requesting exactly that.
- Decision: "You can usually tell within the first three seconds… Something moves. Something
  morphs. The hero scrolls in a way that you've never quite seen before… They're trying to
  impress you, and they're not subtle about it. They've got some kind of interaction almost
  every inch of the website" (11:02–11:25).
- Example (11:25–11:43): **Active Theory** — "the moment that it loads, the type's doing
  something weird, and you scroll, and the sections don't just sit there. They react with
  you as you're scrolling."
- How-to (11:54–12:28): multi-layer skills "from design to UX, some really serious coding
  chops… it typically takes a team to build it"; a one-person show "just… ha[s] to be
  patient and steadily keep adding skills." Tools that shortcut the gap: "**Unicorn Studio**
  or **Spline**… will help any level of designer punch way above their weight."
- Evidence: `t00m10m51` — Inception / Interstellar / The Dark Knight Rises posters (his
  style-anchoring image for Nolan); `t00m11m30` — Active Theory: dark WebGL scene, glass
  torus sculpture with particle spray, chrome shards, pill nav (WORK/CONTACT);
  `t00m12m22` — Spline editor showing a community scene ("Unimodal 2", chrome "NEXUS"
  letters + X-ray brain render, "Remix this scene PRO" chip) — the promised tool demo.
- Effect: memorability and perceived technical mastery; a portfolio differentiator.
- Fails: performance budgets, accessibility, and maintenance cost; long timelines for
  solo builders (his explicit caveats).
- Transfer: reach for Unicorn Studio/Spline-class tools before hand-rolled WebGL; scope
  interactions to a team's real capacity.

### L16-8 Meta: style selection is client-message matching, not preference
- Problem: the video's implicit question — which of the six do I use?
- Decision: each style is introduced with its matching client signal: "I just want
  something clean" → Steve Jobs (1:13); investor-facing stability → Jensen (3:12–3:20);
  "approachable, down to earth" → Drew (6:20); taste-as-value → Zendaya (8:06); "sick"
  signature moment → Virgil (9:20); "wow factor" → Nolan (11:44). Conversely "clients who
  fight me on every decision" are handled by process, not style (4:25–4:55, course pitch).
- Reasoning: [interp] styles are communication strategies mapped to audience psychology —
  he names the audience each style is speaking to in every segment (no explicit summary
  line; the pattern is consistent across all six).
- Evidence: the six segments' quoted client-phrase lines above; title cards as a set.
- Transfer: before opening the builder, classify the client's one-line need into one of the
  six archetypes; that choice then dictates type, layout, imagery, and motion budgets.
