## Lesson log

### L19-1 Run every project as a fixed phase pipeline, mirrored 1:1 on a Trello board
- Problem: ad-hoc client projects stall; steps and client responsibilities are implicit.
- Decision [transcript 0:15–0:36]: every project breaks into four sections — onboarding,
  design and development, launch, offboarding. "That last one might sound like a made up word
  … but it's something that I've seen actually double or even triple the lifelong revenue" (0:23–0:32).
- Evidence: frame `t00m11m41` — the full Trello board "Web Design Project Management" with lists:
  **Project Start** (Welcome! - START HERE / Subscribe to the Project Board / Uploading Files /
  Due Dates / Viewing the Work In Progress / Project Credentials), **Milestone 1: Defining Site
  Goals** (Questionnaire / Functionality [checklist 0/8] / Customer Avatar / Goals: User
  Experience), **Milestone 2: Site Structure Phase** (Sitemap / Wireframe / Content Addition &
  Adjustments), **Milestone 3: Pre-Lim Design Phase** (Your Logo and Branding Materials /
  Website Design Inspiration / Potential Themes / Stock Images for Your Site), **Milestone 4:
  Design Phase** (Home Page / Ancillary Pages / Final Round of Feedback), **Milestone 5: Build,
  De-Bug & Launch** (Issues / Additional Pages / Launch). `t00m0m15` = channel bumper (issue, not the board).
- Implementation: client onboarding is board-native — the client is literally subscribed to the
  project board; deliverables are cards inside milestone lists; the same milestone names are the
  revision gates he recites in speech.
- Effect: one artifact (the board) is the contract chronology, the client's view of progress,
  and his own checklist.
- When appropriate: any client-service design engagement. Fails: fast iterative product work
  with continuous delivery expectations (milestone gates would throttle it).
- Transfer: encode a methodology as a shareable board template with an explicit "START HERE" card.

### L19-2 Onboarding: the discovery call is about the client, not you
- Problem [transcript 0:53–1:02]: designers "end up talking about themselves so much. It's kind
  of like going on a date."
- Decision: make the client "the star of the show" (1:13) — ask about their business, hopes and
  dreams, frustrations with their current website, and frustrations with past freelancers; mine
  that for information used later in the build. Only then check mutual fit.
- Evidence: `t00m8m50` (monospace overlay recapping the discovery-call steps: "Phone call /
  Get project details / See if it's a good fit / Before moving forward") — note this recap card
  is inserted mid-video during the launch segment, a loose edit, but the content matches the
  discovery-call speech at 0:37–1:30.
- Reasoning (explicit 1:23): "You're trying to unearth some really good information that will
  help you as you go along."
- Effect: qualification without pitching; raw material for the questionnaire and site goals.
- Transfer: in any intake conversation (design, dev, consulting), budget the talk ratio;
  capture frustrations verbatim as future requirements.
- [interp] The board's "Customer Avatar" and "Goals: User Experience" cards in Milestone 1
  (`t00m11m41`) are where this interview data lands.

### L19-3 Treat the contract + first payment as the only real project gate
- Problem: warm conversations, lunches, staff tours — then the client ghosts.
- Decision [transcript 1:31–2:21]: discovery → proposal → contract → first payment; "you don't
  have a project until you have a signed contract and a first payment; without those, it's just
  a lovely idea" (1:57–2:02). Also: "beat out 90 percent of your competition if you just respond
  to emails within 48 hours" (1:48–1:52).
- Evidence: `t00m0m40` (mid-transition text card over b-roll defining ONBOARDING as "a discovery
  call, proposal, contract, first payment, [kickoff] call"); `t00m1m35` (kinetic caption moment
  in this stretch).
- Implementation: no work starts before both gates; over-communicate during the proposal phase.
- Effect: eliminates free work and no-show clients; responsiveness itself becomes a differentiator.
- Transfer: same gate logic for any retainer/freelance deal; the pipeline prompt is "what is
  my signed-contract moment?"

### L19-4 Kickoff sets deadlines for BOTH sides — client deadlines carry consequences
- Problem [transcript 2:38–2:53]: "Nothing will derail a project quite like having to wait on a
  client to get you the stuff that you need" — copy, images, branding, feedback.
- Decision: set timelines and deadlines explicitly on the kickoff call, including client-side
  deliverables, "and you give them deadlines with consequences" (2:54) — a fee, or forfeiture of
  that feedback round. Client copy is called out with a kinetic "COPY" caption (2:37, frame
  `t00m2m37`) as one of the deliverables he waits on.
- Evidence: `t00m11m41` — board card "Due Dates" sits in the client-facing Project Start list;
  checklist card "Functionality 0/8" inside Milestone 1.
- Effect: the dependency risk shifts to the client; projects keep a pace.
- Fails: clients who interpret penalties as hostility — he softens this in L19-9's framing
  ("this isn't about sticking it to your clients," 8:13).
- Transfer: put client-obligation dates in the same plan as your own; attach a default
  consequence per round of feedback before the project starts.

### L19-5 The 24-hour revision rule: one round per step, then the round closes
- Problem: open-ended revision cycles kill momentum ("everybody wants their website finished
  yesterday," 8:31).
- Decision [transcript 3:04–3:31 + 7:19–8:38]: clients get exactly 24 hours to respond to each
  deliverable — "If you give them three days, they're going to wait until three days. If you
  give them 24 hours, they're going to do it within 24 hours" (3:20–3:26). One revision per
  stage: wireframes/sitemap, homepage, all other pages (7:22–7:35); feedback → changes → move
  on; "if at any point they're like, Hey, can we go back and change the sitemap? The answer is
  that round of revisions is currently closed" (7:54–8:03). Reopening is possible via an extra
  fee, "or you can just be generous" (8:05–8:16).
- Evidence: `t00m3m05` (plain talking-head beat in the 24-hour passage), `t00m7m25` (same frame
  family), `t00m11m41` (board's "Final Round of Feedback" card names the boundary in Milestone 4).
- Implementation: revision windows are announced at kickoff; close a round on expiry — missed
  window = the feedback waits or is purchased.
- Effect (explicit 8:28): positioning himself as "a little bit of a project manager" whose job
  is "to make sure that things move forward… you're actually helping your clients."
- Fails: genuinely slow-stakeholder clients who need async weeks (a 24h SLA must be agreed, not imposed).
- Transfer: inconveniencing-as-a-feature: tight response windows are a fairness device when
  symmetric and declared up front.

### L19-6 Sequence complexity: sitemap → wireframe → homepage → clone sections to other pages
- Problem [transcript 3:35–4:03]: jumping straight to the homepage "might lead to a lot of
  unnecessary work… and it doesn't allow your client to really be speaking into the process"
  — clients can't articulate what they want until they see something.
- Decision: start with a sitemap (basic layout of all pages and how they connect) and a
  wireframe (lo-fi mockup per page) — then design the homepage, which "typically takes the
  majority of my time" because it "sets the stage for all of the other pages" (5:57–6:02); copy
  its sections to other pages "so you're not having to come up with completely new layouts" (6:11–6:20).
- Evidence: `t00m11m41` — Milestone 2 list literally encodes the order (Sitemap → Wireframe →
  Content Addition & Adjustments) and Milestone 4 splits Home Page from Ancillary Pages;
  `t00m5m53` (desk b-roll; design board visible on his monitor but unreadable at this angle —
  presence only, not cited as design evidence).
- Effect: the homepage becomes a reusable pattern library; per-page design cost collapses.
- Transfer: treat the first/highest-traffic surface as the system; downstream pages are
  recombinations, not new designs.

### L19-7 Go even lower-fidelity than lo-fi: wireframes must make zero design promises
- Problem [transcript 4:20–5:36]: conventional wireframes (rectangle-images, squiggle-text)
  "gotten me into trouble" — a client approved a wireframe with a "really boilerplate font,"
  loved the final design, then asked: "could we use the font that we used in the wireframes"
  (5:17–5:30), and he had to argue against his own artifact.
- Decision: "There's no design decisions being communicated in my wireframes" (4:54–4:58);
  the wireframe is just "this first section on the homepage is going to be about this… the
  second section… the third…" — content-only outlines. Tool: **octopus.do**, including its AI
  mode: "you can tell them what type of website that you're working on and it'll kind of spill
  out a rough idea" (5:39–5:47).
- Evidence: `t00m5m30` — octopus.do canvas: project title "Create a website and wirefra…"
  (+ "TRIAL: 14 DAYS" badge), pink "AI generating… ⃠ Stop" pill, a suggested node "Welcome to
  Our Bakery" with a "Header" segment, page-type dots (Blue/Teal/Purple), bottom-left "Sitemap
  ver. 1", account initials "CM" top-right — i.e., the tool generating a structural outline,
  not styled screens.
- Reasoning (explicit): any given decision in a mockup becomes an anchor the client defends
  later; ambiguity at the structure stage keeps later creative decisions reversible.
- Effect: sign-offs that stick to scope; creative freedom preserved for the design phase.
- Fails: stakeholders who genuinely cannot parse abstract outlines (some clients need more
  concrete artifacts sooner — that's what the staged revisions are for).
- Transfer: match artifact fidelity to the type of feedback you want; never let an
  early-stage artifact decide (by accident) a late-stage concern like type or color.

### L19-8 Never send a naked URL for review — send the reasoning with it
- Problem [transcript 6:21–7:03]: "I don't just send them a URL" — without rationale, clients
  redesign by taste and revisions balloon.
- Decision: every hand-off includes "this is why I put this here, or this is why I used this
  element"; delivery is a **Loom video** walking the design, sometimes plus a live call for
  in-the-moment feedback. "Most clients aren't really going to have an idea for what good
  design is. So there needs to be a reasoning behind your decision" (6:47–6:53).
- Evidence: `t00m10m05` — a Loom recording session (bottom recording bar, 0:06, face-cam
  circle) over a real client site he is editing in the Showit builder (app.showit.com; project
  "My Social Dealer — SOCIALDEALER" site, canvas headline "STAY ON TREND IN SOCIAL MEDIA",
  section "PREMIER SOCIAL SERVICES to help you sell & service more vehicles, profitably" with
  4 icon-cards: Paid Social Advertising / Organic Social Management / Reputation Management /
  Facebook Marketplace; left panel shows SITE/PAGE tabs, Pages: Home, Services, About Us, and
  a BLOG TEMPLATES list). This is the artifact his offboarding Looms are made from.
- Effect: reasons reframe the client's feedback from taste ("I don't like it") to goals
  ("does this achieve what you said?"); fewer revision cycles.
- Transfer: ship a 2-minute narrative with every review artifact; the explanation is part of
  the deliverable, not overhead.

### L19-9 Launch like a QA engineer: dogfood every flow, launch at low-traffic timing
- Problem [transcript 9:09–9:17]: launching broken "only to have to pull it back."
- Decision: client and he pick a launch date "when they don't have a lot of website traffic"
  and when platform support is available (8:48–8:56); then he tests everything himself — fills
  contact forms, **test-buys products**, signs up for his own lead magnets, verifies email
  delivery. "I'm just dog fooding my entire website" (9:06–9:08).
- Evidence: `t00m9m03` (talking head during the testing passage), `t00m11m41` (Milestone 5
  list: Issues → Additional Pages → Launch — a de-bug phase precedes launch).
- Effect: low-risk cutover; no public embarrassment.
- Transfer: define "launch-ready" as having completed each critical flow as a real user,
  including payment.

### L19-10 Offboarding is a product: grace period, FAQ doc, per-client Loom library, then follow-up forever
- Problem [transcript 9:27–9:58]: designers "fail to follow through" after launch; clients are
  suddenly admins of a system they don't know ("How do I log in? How do I add a blog post? How
  do I add a new product?" 9:47–9:52).
- Decision: (1) a support grace period — "just available to jump in and answer questions"
  (9:58–10:05); (2) an FAQ doc (change text, swap images); (3) **Loom videos recorded against
  that specific client's site**, placed on a doc "that they then get to keep for perpetuity…
  almost like a help docs would have on an application or a SAS product" (10:19–10:30); (4)
  then continuous follow-up — remember launch dates, birthdays, business anniversaries, check
  in "at a really comfortable pace" (10:42–11:07).
- Evidence: `t00m10m05` (the Loom recording itself, above); `t00m11m55` (talking head in the
  follow-up/outro stretch — visual only).
- Reasoning (explicit 11:08–11:24): "there's no business like repeat business… some clients
  that have stayed with me for nearly a decade just because I kept following up with them."
  Repeat work even skips discovery: "I don't even have to go through the discovery process
  because I know exactly what they're looking for" (11:37–11:41).
- Effect [transcript, quantified 0:23–0:32]: offboarding "double or even triple[s] the lifelong
  revenue" from one client.
- Fails: one-off transactional engagements with no latent rework demand.
- Transfer: every hand-off should leave behind self-serve documentation modeled on SaaS help
  centers; put follow-ups on a calendar like a growth channel, not as a favor.
