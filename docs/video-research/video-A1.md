## Concept log (timestamps per claim)

1. **Senses as the design surface** [explicit 1:00–1:30] — of the five senses, app
   developers can influence only touch, hearing, and eyesight; all design psychology
   flows through those three channels. Framing device for the whole talk.
2. **Presentation changes perceived value** [presenter-claim 1:51–2:30] — same food
   plated beautifully with vibrant colors is judged more expensive/more appealing than a
   fuller plate presented plainly. Claim: "user interface and the way it's presented to
   you is very very important" (2:26–2:30). The audience poll is slide content; not
   verifiable from transcript.
3. **Beautiful interface = two levers: how much information, in what form** [explicit
   2:44–2:51] — quantity and presentation of information as the two knobs of visual design.
4. **Cognitive overload** [explicit 2:53–3:55] — a dense menu with "so much information…
   at one go" elicits stress on the brain; his definition: "the amount of stress that you
   put on your brain when you're trying to learn new content" (3:20–3:27). Adds that
   "colors were just way too bright" (3:38–3:41) → oversaturated palettes are also
   cognitively taxing (3:43–3:49). Expert note: this definition is looser than the
   cognitive-load-theory sense (intrinsic/extraneous/germane); it is really about
   extraneous load per screen [analyst-inferred].
5. **Processing & perception fluency** [explicit 3:57–4:10] — "the ease with which we can
   process information." presented as the design goal the two levers serve.
6. **Primary feature placement** [presenter-claim 4:10–5:36] — two unnamed "tech giant"
   homepages: one minimal (single search bar centered, two buttons, secondary products
   tucked into a top-right dropdown — recognizably Google), one portal-style (news,
   search, links all at once). "There's nothing wrong with either of the approaches, but
   it's just the way it is presented to you that is different" (5:32–5:36). Lesson he
   draws: the primary function should be obvious from layout; secondary functions
   accessible but recessive.
7. **Color carries learned associations** [explicit 5:40–7:06] — blue = calming
   (sky/sea), green = calm/natural (leaves/fields), red = alert (stop signs, traffic
   lights); therefore notification badges are red: "since it's red, it's going to grab
   your attention and that's why red is being used" (6:58–7:06). Grayscale icons
   "feel" less alive than brand-colored ones (7:09–7:27). T-Mobile litigates over shades
   of magenta — brand color is a guarded asset (7:33–7:53).
8. **Sound + classical conditioning** [presenter-claim 7:55–10:03] — Pavlov experiment
   (dated "1890" in the talk) as the model: neutral stimulus paired with reward until the
   response is involuntary. Phones: ringing triggers an involuntary reach (8:49–9:02);
   notification tones universally "high frequency, high pitch" → alertness (9:30–9:49);
   distinctive tones identify the app without looking (9:51–10:01).
9. **Responsiveness & asymmetric positive reinforcement** [explicit 10:04–10:57] —
   "we are habituated to responsive environments" (10:07–10:12); liking a post fires
   vibration/sound = positive reinforcement (10:24–10:40); disliking a post deliberately
   fires NO sound/vibration "because they do not want to associate that with something
   that you'd want to do" (10:43–10:57). Pleasing behavior gets a reward; unwanted
   behavior gets silence. He calls the former "a good thing" (10:43) without noting the
   manipulation side [analyst note].
10. **Variable reward / "positive intermittent reinforcement"** [explicit 11:00–11:56] —
    pull-to-refresh sometimes yields new content; he maps it to a slot machine and says
    plainly that this is engineered "to create a sort of an addiction" (11:52–11:56).
11. **Personalization via preference capture** [explicit 12:00–13:09] — apps build a
    model of the user from stated and behavioral signals; Spotify asks for five artists
    or three genres at signup to seed the model, then refines from interactions,
    likes/dislikes, playlist adds (12:23–12:56); goal "an app that would be better for
    you particularly" (13:01–13:05).
12. **Hedonic adaptation → novelty cycles** [explicit 13:11–14:50] — users habituate to
    any stimulus (daily ice cream example 13:22–13:40); a happiness "genetic set point"
    reasserts itself (13:49–14:04); Fortnite's frequent season updates exist "so that you
    don't get used to how the app feels" (14:29–14:36) — shipped change is a retention
    tool.
13. **Persuasive design / notification loops** [explicit 14:53–16:01] — design "to
    motivate the users… to behave in a certain way that the developers want" (14:55–15:05);
    Instagram: you post → followers are notified → every reaction on the post notifies
    you back → you reopen the app (15:26–15:59).
14. **Dopamine as the shared target** [presenter-claim 16:04–16:24] — every sensory
    technique "is to ultimately target the dopamine system"; his simplification:
    "the chemical that is released to make us feel happy… if you feel happy about using
    an app, you would use it more." (Dopamine is better described as signaling reward
    prediction/anticipation than "happiness" — pop-science compression.)
15. **Social media addiction cycle** [presenter-claim 16:29–17:00] — action (post) →
    wait → anticipation of reaction → compulsive reopening; he opens Instagram "10 times
    more" on days he posts (16:46–16:55).
16. **Attention economy + closing warning** [explicit 17:03–17:39] — old phones: calls,
    SMS, Snake; now "100 other apps… competing for your attention" (17:17–17:21).
    Closing quote: "There are only two industries that call their customers users,
    illegal drugs and software." (17:32–17:39).

## Techniques / workflows

- Senses-first audit: enumerate which of touch/vision/sound a screen uses and what
  response each is engineered to elicit (talk's organizing method, 1:00–1:30).
- Before/after contrast demos: dense menu vs calm; different presentation of equal
  functionality (4:10–5:36).
- Behavioral-loop decomposition: trigger → action → variable reward → reinforcement
  (his slot-machine mapping, 11:26–11:56; notification loop 15:26–15:59) — a
  habit-loop map done informally, no Hook-model vocabulary [analyst-inferred link].
- Enumeration of asymmetry as a design decision: reward the wanted action, ignore the
  unwanted one (10:24–10:57).

## Product design lessons (Quadly-relevant)

- Join flow (email + password + confirm, per AGENTS.md): only 2–3 decisions on one
  screen — the talk's cognitive-overload lens says keep it that way; never add a field
  that isn't used later (feedback rule pairs with A4's smart defaults).
- Onboarding profiles: apply R-A1-06 — every question asked at onboarding must visibly
  change something the user sees afterward (ambassador matching is exactly the
  Spotify-artists pattern: stated preference → model → better suggestions). If a
  collected answer never surfaces again, the screen fails R-A1-06.
- Feedback asymmetry: success confirmations get celebratory feedback; removing/
  destroying things (deleting a saved profile, cancelling) gets neutral, sober UI.
- Quadly as an evaluator agent must judge engagement features with an explicit ethics
  config: variable-reward and re-engagement loops are checkable but require a policy
  flag deciding whether they are legitimate or dark patterns (R-A1-08) — this talk
  supplies the mechanics and, via its closing quote, the warning.

## Open questions

- Can "stress on your brain" be operationalized enough for automated judgment, or must
  R-A1-04 be proxied by chunk counts / Miller-style item budgets? Needs cross-ref with
  A3 (UX design psychology) for a harder definition.
- Where exactly is the line in R-A1-08? A1 celebrates the loops and quotes the drugs
  comparison without stating criteria; the evaluator needs an external ethics rubric.
- Do sound/haptic layers have a web equivalent strong enough to check, or do rules
  R-A1-02/03 collapse into visual-state checks on the web? (A5's state checklist is the
  visual proxy.)
- How much novelty (R-A1-07) is healthy before "changed for change's sake" hurts
  learnability (A2's repetition rule pulls in the opposite direction)?
