## Concepts (each cited to timestamp)

1. **Aesthetic usability effect** [0:25–1:22]. Attractive things are perceived as easier/better, even when functionally identical (lemonade glass demo [0:29–0:51]); "things that look nice appeal more to the viewers" [0:51–0:54]. Spotify's UI + lyric-share color-matching [0:55–1:10]; Apple's visual consistency → loyalty [1:13–1:22].
2. **Doherty threshold** [1:22–2:01]. "Everyone hates waiting even if it's for a few milliseconds" [1:25–1:32]; keep waits short; where unavoidable, show a progress bar or skeleton screen. LinkedIn skeleton load [1:40–1:51]; YouTube pre-play animation [1:52–2:01].
3. **Fitts's law** [2:03–2:29]. "Bigger and nearer objects are easier to click and interact with" [2:06–2:09]; "make the vital UI components large enough… and give ample spacing" [2:11–2:16]; thumb-zone placement of most-used apps at the bottom of phone screens [2:22–2:27].
4. **Goal-gradient effect** [2:29–3:05]. "The closer you are to completing a goal or a task, the faster you work towards reaching it" [2:47–2:51]; Amazon checkout step markers as accelerant [2:54–3:03].
5. **Hick's law** [3:05–3:46]. "When people have too many choices they often get confused and overwhelmed, which leads to a slower decision process" [3:17–3:21]; "less is more" [3:24]; Google 1998 (link-cluttered) vs 2024 (minimal) homepage [3:27–3:44].
6. **Jakob's law** [3:47–4:28]. "People spend most of their time in other apps and websites, so they expect your apps and websites to work in a similar way" [3:49–3:56]; "don't try to be too unique and break common patterns" [3:58–4:03]; red-for-errors convention [4:05–4:15]; familiar e-commerce nav [4:17–4:27].
7. **Law of similarity** (Gestalt) [4:29–4:45]. "Elements that are visually similar will be perceived as related" [4:29–4:33]; Amazon add-address form: bordered rectangles read as input fields, solid-filled rectangles read as buttons [4:33–4:44].
8. **Miller's law** [4:46–5:28]. Working memory/processing are limited [5:01–5:06]; "don't overload people with too much stuff to process. Split tasks into logical easy to process steps so that users are not overwhelmed" [5:06–5:14]; Amazon/Flipkart checkout broken into name → address → review → payment [5:15–5:27].
9. **Zeigarnik effect** [5:28–5:47]. "People remember unfinished tasks better than completed ones. Provide a clear indication of progress in order to motivate users to complete the task" [5:28–5:36]; Udemy course progress bar [5:38–5:47].
10. **Von Restorff effect** (isolation effect) [5:47–6:11]. "Just like a purple cow easily stands out in a herd, unique and different items stand out… likely to be remembered more" [5:50–5:59]; SaaS pricing pages highlighting the "most popular" plan [5:59–6:10].
11. **Law of Prägnanz** [6:11–6:45]. "The human eye is lazy… it perceives complex shapes and images in the simplest possible form because it requires the least amount of brain cells" [6:12–6:23]; IKEA's wordless step-by-step diagrams — cut to essentials, no translation needed [6:24–6:43].
12. **Tesler's law** (conservation of complexity) [6:45–7:19]. "While some task may seem unnecessarily complex, this complexity is integral to the function of the system and can only be managed and not eliminated" [6:45–7:00]; flight booking requires many fields — cannot remove them, but can manage them ("maybe pre-fill some of the information based on… what you might have entered in the past") [7:01–7:19].

## Product design lessons

- Beauty is a usability signal, not decoration — invest in aesthetics early (AUE).
- Never show a naked wait: skeletons, bars, pre-play animations (Doherty).
- Make the important things big, near, and spaced (Fitts); near the goal people speed up — show the goal (goal-gradient).
- Constrain choices deliberately; novelty must justify itself (Hick, Jakob); look-alike things act alike (Similarity).
- Chunk long processes; show progress on open loops (Miller, Zeigarnik).
- One loud element per screen (Von Restorff); simplest legible form wins (Prägnanz); complexity can only be moved, never deleted — manage, pre-fill (Tesler).
